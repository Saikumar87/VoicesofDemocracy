Democracy at the Grassroots: Local Self-Government in India

Understanding how citizens participate in democracy beyond national elections.

Indian democracy does not operate only in Parliament or state legislatures. It also takes shape in villages, towns and cities, where local institutions make decisions about roads, water, sanitation, schools and community development.

The constitutional foundation

The 73rd and 74th Constitutional Amendment Acts, passed in 1992, gave constitutional recognition to rural and urban local government. The 73rd Amendment concerns Panchayati Raj institutions, while the 74th concerns municipalities and other urban local bodies.

These reforms sought to make government more accessible by creating elected institutions closer to the people. They also provided for regular elections, reservations for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes, and at least one-third reservation for women in these local bodies under the constitutional framework.

What is a Gram Sabha?

A Gram Sabha consists of people registered on the electoral rolls of a village or a group of villages within a Panchayat area. It provides a forum for residents to discuss local needs, review plans and question decisions.

Its practical influence varies across states and communities. Participation can be affected by awareness, social inequalities, access to information and the willingness of local authorities to respond.

Panchayats and municipalities

Rural local government generally has village, intermediate and district-level institutions, although the intermediate tier is not required in states with populations below the constitutional threshold. Urban local bodies include municipal corporations, municipalities and nagar panchayats.

The Constitution identifies areas of responsibility in the Eleventh and Twelfth Schedules. These include subjects such as agriculture, drinking water, roads, public health, urban planning and sanitation. Actual powers and funding depend substantially on state laws and devolution.

Why local democracy matters

  • It gives residents a closer point of contact with elected representatives.
  • It can help communities identify local priorities.
  • It creates opportunities to scrutinise public spending and services.
  • It provides a route for women and historically marginalised communities to hold elected office.

Questions for citizens

Who decides which local roads are repaired? How can residents see a Panchayat's development plans? Are Gram Sabha meetings accessible to women, workers and marginalised groups? These are practical questions through which citizens can assess the health of local democracy.
